Stirling engine (lab model): interactive model

The model below is the transparent Stirling engine used in the lab exercise (the displacement piston and the working piston move in two separate cylinders). An alcohol burner heats the rounded end of the glass cylinder, and the gas gives off heat at the cold end near the frame. Part of the heat is turned into work, which drives the flywheel and the generator. The numbers in brackets are the part numbers in the manufacturer's drawing. Rotate the model and click on a part to read what it does. Use the sliders to change the operating conditions.

About this model. This page is for teaching. It combines the ideal textbook Stirling cycle with a simplified isothermal model of continuous piston motion. The pressure, work and power shown are theoretical estimates, not measurements of the real engine. A real engine gives less, because of friction, flow losses and imperfect heat transfer.

2. The four steps of the cycle

The air inside is sealed in. The displacement piston A only pushes the gas between the hot end and the cold end of the glass cylinder. The working piston B feels the pressure. Hot gas pushes hard, cold gas pushes weakly. Expanding while hot and compressing while cold leaves extra work over each turn, and that turns the flywheel. Click a step to jump there. The pictures refer to Fig. 1 in the manual (drawn with the hot end at the top).

Step 1→2 · Fig. 1, picture 3

Compress while cold

Most of the gas is at the cold end. The flywheel pushes the working piston B into its cylinder. Cold gas is easy to squeeze, so this costs only a little work. Heat leaves through the cold end.

T = Tc constant, V ↓, p ↑
WV,12 > 0 (work in)
Q12 < 0 (heat out)
Step 2→3 · Fig. 1, picture 4

Move gas to the hot end

The displacement piston A moves towards the cold end and pushes the gas around it to the hot end. The volume stays about the same, but the gas heats up, so the pressure jumps.

V ≈ constant, T ↑ ⇒ p ↑↑
Q23 > 0 (heat in)
WV,23 = 0
Step 3→4 · Fig. 1, picture 1

Expand while hot: the power stroke

High-pressure hot gas pushes the working piston B out. This is where the engine does work and drives the crank, flywheel and generator.

T = Th constant, V ↑, p ↓
WV,34 < 0 (work out)
Q34 > 0 (heat in)
Step 4→1 · Fig. 1, picture 2

Move gas to the cold end

The displacement piston A moves towards the hot end and pushes the gas back to the cold end. The gas cools and the pressure drops, ready to be compressed cheaply again.

V ≈ constant, T ↓ ⇒ p ↓↓
Q41 < 0 (heat out)
WV,41 = 0

Why 90°? The crank of A is about a quarter turn ahead of the crank of B. So the gas is already hot when B is ready to move out, and already cold when B is ready to move in. In Fig. 1 you can see this: in each picture one piston moves while the other is near its turning point.

3. Calculations

Sign convention

The system is the gas inside the engine. Everything is counted from the gas's point of view:

  • Heat or work going into the gas is positive (+).
  • Heat or work coming out of the gas is negative (−).
ΔU = Q + WV,    WV = −∫ p dV

Compression (dV < 0) gives WV > 0: the flywheel puts work in. Expansion (dV > 0) gives WV < 0: the gas gives work out. Over a full cycle ΔU = 0, so WV = −ΣQ. A working engine therefore has a negative net WV.

The green boxes are calculated from the slider values above. Engine size used here (chosen to give the manufacturer's gas volume of 32–44 cm³; the real piston sizes may differ):

3.1

Absolute temperature

Gas laws only work with absolute temperature. Absolute zero (0 K) is the lowest possible temperature.

T [K] = T [°C] + 273.15
3.2

Ideal gas law

For a fixed amount of gas, pressure, volume and temperature are linked:

p · V = m · R · T   ⇒   p = m R TV

p = pressure (Pa), V = volume (m³), m = mass of air (kg), R = 287 J/(kg·K) for air, T = temperature (K).
Read it like this: hotter air → higher pressure (same volume), and smaller volume → higher pressure (same temperature).

3.3

Swept volumes

A piston moving a distance s in a cylinder of diameter d sweeps a cylinder of volume:

Vswept = π d²4 · s

The compression ratio is about 1.4 (gas volume 32 → 44 cm³). That is small compared with a car engine (ε ≈ 10), but normal for hot-air engines: the work comes from the temperature difference, not from big compression.

3.4

Mass of air in the engine

The engine was sealed at room temperature (20 °C = 293.15 K) and atmospheric pressure (101 325 Pa). Rearranging the gas law:

m R = p0 V0T0     m = p0 V0R T0
3.5

Heat and work in the four ideal steps

Sections 3.5–3.8 are for the ideal cycle, the dashed loop in the p–V diagram. The orange oval (the isothermal model) follows in 3.9.

In the textbook cycle, steps 1 and 3 happen at constant temperature (isothermal) and steps 2 and 4 at constant volume (isochoric). With r = Vmax/Vmin:

①  WV,12 = +m R Tc ln r > 0,  Q12 = −WV,12 < 0
②  Q23 = m cv (Th − Tc) > 0,  WV = 0
③  WV,34 = −m R Th ln r < 0,  Q34 = −WV,34 > 0
④  Q41 = m cv (Tc − Th) < 0,  WV = 0

Where does the "ln" come from? At constant temperature, p = mRT/V, so WV = −∫p dV = −mRT ∫ dV/V = −mRT ln(V2/V1). In compression V2/V1 = 1/r, so ln is negative and WV is positive. In expansion it is the other way round.
At constant temperature an ideal gas has ΔU = 0, so Q = −WV: during compression the heat leaves through the fins, and during expansion it comes in from the flame. At constant volume WV = 0, so ΔU = Q = m cv ΔT.
Heat from step ④ can be stored in the regenerator and given back in step ②. In this engine there is no separate regenerator: the displacer wall and the glass along the narrow gap take up some heat from the gas and give part of it back, so they act as a weak regenerator. In a perfect engine, the only heat bought from the flame is the heat in step ③. Notice in the table how large the regenerator heat is compared with the work. That's why a good regenerator matters so much.

3.6

Net work (ideal cycle)

Add the compression work (positive, in) to the expansion work (negative, out):

WV = WV,12 + WV,34 = −m R (Th − Tc) · ln VmaxVmin < 0

The result is negative because more work comes out of the gas than goes in. That's what makes it an engine. The area of the dashed loop equals |WV|. Check with the first law: over a cycle ΔU = 0, so WV = −(Q12 + Q23 + Q34 + Q41).
No temperature difference (Th = Tc) means WV = 0. A larger temperature difference increases the theoretical net work per cycle. Depending on friction and the load, this may let the engine run faster or deliver more power. (On this page the speed is a slider, not a result.)

3.7

Efficiency (ideal cycle)

Efficiency = work out ÷ heat in. Because WV is negative, we write −WV to get a positive number. With a perfect regenerator, steps ② and ④ cancel, so the only heat bought from the flame is Q34:

η = −WVQ34 = 1 + Q12Q34 = 1 + −mR Tc ln rmR Th ln r = 1 − TcTh

This is the same form as η = 1 + qout/qin for Otto, Diesel and Carnot in the slides. The heat out is negative, so it lowers the efficiency. The ideal Stirling cycle gives the Carnot efficiency because two conditions are met: a perfect regenerator, so all the bought heat enters at Th and all the rejected heat leaves at Tc, and reversible processes (no friction, heat exchanged without temperature differences).

Try it: how much does the regenerator matter? With a regenerator effectiveness εr (0 = none, 1 = perfect), the flame must also supply the part of Q23 the regenerator does not return:

η = m R (Th − Tc) ln rm R Th ln r + (1 − εr) m cv (Th − Tc)

The work per cycle does not change, only the heat that must be bought. So a better regenerator raises the efficiency, not the work.

3.8

Indicated power (ideal cycle)

Pind = −WV · f,    f = rpm60

The minus sign turns the negative work per turn into a positive power. This is the indicated power: the power the gas delivers to the pistons, worked out from the p–V loop. The shaft power at the crank is lower because of friction, and the electrical power at the bulb is lower again because of generator losses. It also assumes the work per turn does not depend on speed; in a real engine, faster running leaves less time for heat transfer.

3.9

The isothermal (Schmidt-type) model: area of the orange oval

In this model both pistons move all the time, so the four steps overlap and there is no formula for each step. Instead, the page turns the crank through one full turn in 720 small steps. At each step it takes the volume V from the piston positions and the pressure p from the gas law, with the gas split between the hot space, the cold space and the gap:

p = m RVh/Th + Vc/Tc + Vr/Tr

Then it adds up thin strips around the loop: for each small step, the average pressure times the change in volume.

WV = −∮ p dV ≈ −Σ pavg ΔV,    Pind = −WV · f

On the way out (ΔV > 0) the strips count one way and on the way back (ΔV < 0) the other, so what is left is the area inside the oval. The same method would be used on a measured loop, if p and V were recorded with a pressure sensor and a position sensor.
The oval is much smaller than the dashed loop because the pistons never stop, so the steps overlap, and because the gas is always split between the hot and cold spaces, so the pressure swings less. The model still assumes the same pressure everywhere, fixed gas temperatures in the hot and cold spaces, an ideal gas, no flow losses, no friction and perfect heat transfer, so a real engine gives less again.

Three levels of model: ① the ideal textbook cycle (dashed loop, 3.5–3.8) → ② the isothermal model with moving pistons (orange oval, here) → ③ the real engine with friction and heat-transfer losses (smaller still, not shown on this page).
